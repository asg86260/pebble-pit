Language picker and the CJK look

Mocks for DESIGN.md, "Localization phase two (design, not built)": section 1 is where the language is chosen, section 2 is how Chinese, Japanese and Korean sit in the house type. Nothing here is game code; vote, then it gets built.

This is a mock, drawn from plain HTML, not the game. The sheets reuse the game's own CSS rules, copied from src/style.css and src/title.css. The two knobs section 2 turns, --track and --step, are the design's proposal, and both are 1 and 0 (the game's numbers) everywhere in section 1.
  • What it cannot show: the yard behind the sheets, the held sheet's wash, the phone's bottom-sheet seat and drag handle, the real pad marker's halo and glide, and the reload a language change costs (only a white veil stands in for it). Each panel is always light or dark with the page; the game's own dark switch is not wired up.
  • The faces: the CJK glyphs come from Google's Noto Sans SC/JP/KR, standing in for the subset faces the design would bundle. Latin text is whatever ui-monospace, "Courier New" your machine has, as in the game.
  • The words: anything in grey italics in brackets is a [owner's words] placeholder. The CJK lines are the machine-drafted pilot (docs/l10n-pilot/draft/), not approved translations. locales/zh-CN.ftl etc. do not exist on main yet. The pilot does not have "paused", "resume" or "settings", so the CJK sheet in section 2 is put together from drafted lines on several pages.

1. The picker

Each option is shown on the held sheet's settings page (play.html) and on the landing page's settings (index.html). The language in force is English, marked by inverting it, the way the boards mark the brew on the boil and the saves mark the open yard. The purple corners are where the d-pad's marker would stand. Click the rows; they work.

All three put the language in force in the landing page's foot as well, beside itch · github, in its own name, as the design asks. Clicking it opens the picker.

One button on settings, beside dark. It opens a page of nine rows, each in its own name, with a back button. This is how controls already works.

Controller
D-pad down to the row, A opens the page. Up and down walk the nine, A picks one (the page goes white and reloads in that language), B or the back row returns. The page opens with the marker on the language in force. Nothing new for padpage.js: every row is a button.
Phone
Nine full-width tap targets on a page of their own, about 320px of rows at 12px. That fits a phone's held sheet without scrolling, and settings grows by only one row.
Cost
One more page (a data-pane), three placeholder messages. A player who cannot read the current language has to find the right row first. The foot link and a row label that names the current language (e.g. [language] · English) both help there.

(b) One row that cycles, like the on/off switches

One button, [language]: English. Each press moves to the next of the nine. In the game every press reloads the page, the way dark does, so stepping from English to Korean means eight presses and eight white flashes. Press it to see the veil.

Controller
D-pad to the row, A steps forward. Left and right could step both ways, but padpage.js gives left and right only to a slider today, so that would be new. Each step is a reload, or the switch waits a beat before it reloads, which no other switch on the sheet does.
Phone
The smallest footprint: settings does not grow at all. Up to eight taps, each a reload, to reach the far end of the list. A player who is lost in an unreadable language taps blindly until a script they recognize comes round.
Cost
The least markup. It is the shape the design already rejected for nine choices: a switch reads as on/off, and nine states behind one word are hidden until you step through them.

(c) The list inline on the settings page

Nine rows in a box on the settings page itself, under a small heading. The box is the index page's list (tight rows, chosen one inverted), so it reads as one list rather than nine more menu buttons.

Controller
Every row is a stop, so the d-pad walks nine language rows to get from dark to sound, controls and back, on every visit to settings. A is a pick and a reload, so a press meant for the row below costs a reload into the wrong language.
Phone
Rows only about 17px tall, under a comfortable thumb target unless they grow, and if they grow the settings page stops fitting. The held sheet's settings goes from about 330px to about 520px, which scrolls in landscape. The landing column runs past its frame (shown here at 640px tall).
Cost
No new page, and the choice is always visible. It puts a once-ever setting in the way of the settings people change often.

2. The CJK look

The same composite held sheet and a board card, in Chinese, Japanese and Korean, under three treatments. In every treatment the face is the game's stack with the CJK face after it, so Latin letters, digits and the coin marks keep the game's mono and only the ideographs, kana and hangul fall through to Noto. English is shown once first for reference; none of the treatments touch it.

Lines from the draft: saved-word, saved-why, saved-keepplaying, set-dark-on, set-motion-less, held-nevermind, held-keys, job-rockhands, row-carry, row-autotoss, unit-per-s, kit-breaker-does, card-done, obj-rock, row-dome-note. The time, the bill and the pips are made up.

English, for reference (the game today)

(a) As today: capitals (no effect on CJK) and the wide tracking kept

--track: 1, --step: 0. The sheet's word is tracked .3em, buttons .14em, cards .1em, and all of it at 10 to 17px.

(b) Tracking off for CJK, same size

--track: 0 under :lang(zh|ja|ko). Korean also takes word-break: keep-all.

(c) Tracking off and a step larger, Latin numbers and coins kept in the mono at today's size

--track: 0, --step: 2px (12 → 14, sheet word 17 → 19, small 10 → 12). The bill line and the numbers stay at 12px in the mono.

What stands in for the capitals: heading emphasis

In Latin, a heading is marked by capitals plus wide tracking. In CJK both are gone, so the sheet's word and a board's section heading need another mark. Every option below is on treatment (c), with the English (capitals) at the left for comparison. The badge keeps the mono.

Two things the shots show that no treatment fixes. In Korean the spaces between words, and the ? and !, come from the Latin mono, because the CJK face stands after it in the stack. A mono space is a full cell, so Korean words sit far apart (끝난 건가?). Putting the Korean face first for spaces and punctuation, or using unicode-range on the bundled face, would close that. Second, a card's name still runs under its pips when it is long, in Korean (누르고 있으면 던지기) as in English. The game clips it with an ellipsis in the same place; that is the length rule in the translators' brief, not a type setting.
